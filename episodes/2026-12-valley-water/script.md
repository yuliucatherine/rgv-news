## COLD OPEN (0:00–0:55)
[VISUAL: Blender flyover of Falcon Reservoir. The water plane sinks from full to today's level while a counter ticks the year from 2010 to 2026.]
**N00:** In October of twenty ten, Falcon Reservoir on the Rio Grande was completely full. One hundred percent.
**N01:** Fourteen years later, it had fallen to thirteen percent. Today, it sits at about twenty-three.
[VISUAL: animated line chart of Falcon's daily level, 1990 to today, drawing itself left to right]
**N02:** Almost every faucet, field, and factory in the Lower Rio Grande Valley depends on this river and on two reservoirs upstream. And the Valley is growing fast: new subdivisions, new plants, a rocket company building next door.
*KINETIC: WILL · THERE · BE · ENOUGH?*
**N03:** So for anyone who builds, buys, or lends here, the question is simple. Will there be enough water to grow? The answer is more surprising than you might think. This is RGV News.

## PART 1: WHERE THE WATER COMES FROM (0:55–2:45)
[VISUAL: map zooms from Texas to the Rio Grande basin. Amistad and Falcon are highlighted, and the river glows as it flows to the Gulf. Labels for Del Rio, Laredo, Falcon, McAllen, Brownsville.]
**N04:** The Valley's water starts far upstream. Two big reservoirs on the Rio Grande, Amistad near Del Rio and Falcon below Laredo, store the supply that flows down to the Valley's cities and irrigation districts.
**N04b:** In Brownsville, about seventy percent of the city's water comes from the Rio Grande. Across the Valley, the river is the backbone of the supply for cities and farms alike.
(Texas Tribune via KRGV, Aug 31, 2026: Brownsville about 21M gal/day, 70% from the Rio Grande)
**N05:** Those reservoirs are shared with Mexico. And under a treaty signed in nineteen forty-four, Mexico owes the United States a share of the water from its rivers that feed the Rio Grande, counted over five-year cycles.
[VISUAL: animated treaty timeline card]
**N06:** For years, Mexico fell behind. When the last cycle closed in October of twenty twenty-five, it ended deep in debt. In early twenty twenty-six, after pressure from Washington, Mexico committed to deliver at least three hundred and fifty thousand acre-feet a year, and to repay what it owes.
**N07:** This October, the first year of the new cycle closed, and Mexico says it met that minimum. That's real progress. But by most estimates, it still owes more than eight hundred thousand acre-feet from the last cycle.
*ON SCREEN: Mexico, year 1 of the new cycle: minimum met (350,000 acre-feet) · Still owed from the last cycle: about 830,000 to 860,000 acre-feet*
(Rio Times / Texas Politics, Oct 2026; Freshplaza; Sen. Cruz's office; State Dept. joint statement, Feb 2026; Baker Institute)
**N08:** An acre-foot, by the way, is enough water to cover a football field about nine inches deep, or roughly what two to three Valley households use in a year.
[VISUAL: 3D football field filling with water]

## PART 2: THE RESERVOIRS (2:45–4:00)
[VISUAL: dual 3D columns, Falcon and Amistad, rising and falling by year, 2010 → 2024 → 2026, with counting labels]
**N09:** Here's what the last fifteen years looked like. Both reservoirs were full in twenty ten. Then came a long drought.
**N10:** In July of twenty twenty-four, Amistad hit its lowest level in at least three decades, about twenty-four percent. Falcon spent most of twenty twenty-three and twenty twenty-four below fifteen percent.
**N11:** This fall brought some relief. Amistad has climbed back to about fifty-three percent, and Falcon to about twenty-three. Better than a year ago. But nowhere near healthy.
*ON SCREEN: Today: Amistad 53% · Falcon 23% (Water Data for Texas, Nov 17, 2026)*
(Water Data for Texas daily records: Amistad minimum since 1990 = 24.0% on 2024-07-07; Falcon annual means 14% in 2023 and 2024)

## PART 3: WHO GETS CUT FIRST (4:00–5:45)
*KINETIC: SO · WHO · RUNS · OUT?*
**N12:** So here's the surprising part. When water gets short in the Valley, cities are not first in line to lose it.
**N13:** The water rights system in the Amistad-Falcon reservoir system protects municipal water. Irrigation rights are the ones that get cut in a drought.
[VISUAL: document card, highlighted lines from the 2026 Rio Grande Regional Water Plan: "Region M does not have municipal unmet needs" and "municipal water rights are protected, and irrigation water rights have lower reliability"]
**N14:** The region's own twenty twenty-six water plan says it plainly: the Valley does not have municipal unmet needs. Farms do. In a severe drought, irrigation in the region could come up short by about eight hundred and eighty thousand acre-feet a year by twenty thirty.
*ON SCREEN: Irrigation shortfall in drought, 2030: Hidalgo about 431,000 ac-ft · Cameron about 362,000 · Willacy about 76,000*
(2026 Rio Grande Regional Water Plan (Region M), Ch. 6, Table 6-2)
**N15:** We've already seen what that looks like. In twenty twenty-four, the Valley's sugar mill, the last one in Texas, shut down after fifty years, citing heat and a shortage of irrigation water. Hundreds of workers were laid off. The state is now trying to revive it.
(KSAT, Mar 1, 2024; Texas Workforce Commission notice: 435 layoffs; KRGV on the Santa Rosa Sugar revival)
**N15b:** But the line between farm water and city water isn't as clean as it sounds. Across much of the Valley, water for towns travels through the same irrigation canals that serve the fields.
[VISUAL: Blender canal network glowing across a map of Hidalgo and Cameron counties, with town markers fed by canal lines]
**N15c:** Raymondville, for example, still gets its Rio Grande water delivered through the Delta Lake Irrigation District. Its general manager says the district is running at a fraction of its former capacity.
(KRGV, irrigation district canal funding report, 2026)
**N15d:** And those canals need enough water flowing through them to push deliveries down the line. The regional plan warns that as farm water rights are converted away, there's less of that push water, so more has to be diverted to get the same amount to a city's tap. That's one reason fixing the canals matters so much.
(2026 Region M plan, Sec. 6.1.7)

## PART 4: WHAT IT MEANS FOR BUILDERS (5:45–8:00)
*KINETIC: FARM · WATER · BECOMES · CITY · WATER*
**N16:** So if cities are protected, where does the water for new neighborhoods come from? Largely from farms.
**N17:** As farmland becomes subdivisions, the water rights attached to that land can be converted from irrigation to municipal use. But not one for one. Under state rules, it takes about two acre-feet of irrigation rights to make one acre-foot of city water.
[VISUAL: animated conversion graphic, two blue farm blocks merging into one city block]
(2026 Region M plan, Sec. 6.1.7: TCEQ conversion ratios, 2 ac-ft Class A or 2.5 ac-ft Class B irrigation to 1 ac-ft municipal)
**N18:** The regional plan expects tens of thousands of irrigated acres to give way to urban growth over the coming decades, more than ten thousand in Hidalgo County alone by twenty thirty.
[VISUAL: Blender map of Hidalgo and Cameron farmland with urban tiles spreading]
*ON SCREEN: Irrigated acres lost to urbanization (projected): Hidalgo 10,155 by 2030 → 60,521 by 2080 · Cameron 6,948 → 41,409*
(Region M plan, Table 6-1)
**N19:** For developers, that makes water a cost and a timing question. In some Valley cities, proving that a project has its own water supply is a condition of building. Others have been rethinking their rules.
**N20:** In twenty twenty-four, Mission considered pausing new developments larger than five acres because of water scarcity, then stepped back at the last minute. This spring, it briefly paused new car washes, until a lawsuit ended that moratorium.
(MyRGV, Mar 20, 2024; KRGV, Mar–Apr 2026)
**N21:** Meanwhile, the growth keeps coming. Edinburg says it has at least a hundred and twenty-seven subdivisions under construction, and its mayor has promised an independent water supply by twenty thirty.
*QUOTE: "Water security is a top priority... we need to think ahead." (Edinburg Mayor Omar Ochoa, to the RGV Business Journal, Apr 2026)*
**N21b:** The city is studying desalination of brackish groundwater, and capturing stormwater before it drains into the Rio Grande and out to the Gulf. It's also spending thirty-three million dollars to expand its wastewater treatment plant, from about twelve million gallons a day to thirteen and a half.
(RGV Business Journal, Apr 10, 2026)
**N22:** And in Brownsville, as we covered in our last episode, SpaceX's land deal is paying for a water reuse plant and a treatment-plant expansion. Water is now part of how big projects get negotiated.

## PART 5: NEW WATER (8:00–9:30)
*KINETIC: WHERE · NEW · WATER · COMES · FROM*
**N23:** The good news: there's real money moving to create new supply.
**N24:** Statewide, Texas voters approved Proposition 4 in twenty twenty-five, about seventy percent in favor. It dedicates up to a billion dollars a year in sales tax to a new state water fund, starting in twenty twenty-seven.
(Texas Tribune; Texas Secretary of State; Texas 2036)
**N25:** Closer to home, a proposed package of up to a hundred and sixty-four million dollars would turn leaky open canals into pipelines across twelve irrigation districts, saving up to forty-four thousand acre-feet a year. Some districts are already being funded.
(NADBank, Aug 27, 2026; TWDB Board item, Sep 10, 2026; KRGV)
**N26:** And on South Padre Island, a private venture is planning a billion-dollar seawater desalination plant, fifty million gallons a day. That's roughly fifty-six thousand acre-feet a year, a meaningful new source. But it would be privately owned, with the company controlling contracts and prices, and many steps remain before it breaks ground.
(KRGV; Texas Border Business; MyRGV, Aug 11, 2026; Smart Water Magazine)
[VISUAL: Blender coastline flyover of South Padre with an illustrative plant marker labeled "Proposed site (illustrative)"]

## PART 6: WHAT TO WATCH (9:30–10:45)
**N27:** So what does this mean if you build, buy, or lend in the Valley?
**N28:** The tap is not likely to run dry in town. Cities are protected first. The bigger risks are cost, timing, and rules: the price of new water, the rights a project may need to bring with it, and how fast cities change their development policies when the reservoirs drop.
*LIST: Risks to watch · Reservoir levels and Mexico's deliveries · City water-rights rules for new projects · The price of new water (desalination, reuse) · Moratorium talk during droughts*
**N29:** Before you buy land or start a project, ask three questions. Who supplies water to this site, a city, a water supply corporation, or an irrigation district? Does the project need to bring its own water rights, and at what cost? And what's that supplier's drought plan when Falcon falls again?
*LIST: Ask before you build (3 questions)*
**N29b:** There's an opportunity side too. Projects that come with their own water, through converted rights, reuse, or efficient design, may move faster when cities tighten up. Builders who know the water rules can spot land that others overlook, or avoid deals that look cheap until the water bill arrives.
**N29c:** And the money now flowing into canals, reuse plants, and desalination means construction and engineering work across the Valley for years to come, whatever the reservoirs do.
**N30:** The Valley has water to grow. But it's not free, and it's not automatic. The projects that plan for water first will be the ones that pencil out. This is RGV News.
